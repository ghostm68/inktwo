San Francisco, 1937 – the year the bay fog learned to wear lipstick and carry a stiletto under its garters. In the Black Cat Cellar on Grant Avenue, the air was thick enough to carve with a bread knife, reeking of damp corduroy, burnt coffee, and illicit peppermint gin.

"You’re late, Quill," she said without turning around. "And when you’re late, people start counting brass casings instead of drinks."

Mr. Quill slid into the corner booth, his trench coat still glistening with Pacific drizzle. The saxophone player on the bandstand leaned back, his reed wheezing a tune that sounded like broken glass sliding down a velvet banister.

Lola Lazar tilted her chin, the cigarette smoke curling like a cobra around her collarbone. Her satin gown was the color of bruised plums—the kind of dress that makes a cathedral bell forget how to chime.

"Murder is my native tongue, dollface," Quill murmured, tapping a crumpled telegram against the mahogany table. "What’s yours?"

She opened her purse. Not for powder, and not for money. Inside lay a snub-nosed .32 wrapped in an oilskin handkerchief, cold as a December tide off Alcatraz. The Satin Doll was going down, and everyone in the room was holding an alibi that smelled like kerosene.

The Cord 810 roared along the pier cranes, its supercharged pipes tearing through the salt air. The pavement was slick with fish oil and rain. Behind them, two sets of yellow headlights sliced through the mist, keeping pace like twin predators on a scent trail.

"One slug in the engine block, one slug in the drum," she yelled over the supercharger whine. "Hold the wheel!"

Craddock’s men were closing in from the wharf. The tires screeched across wet railroad spurs as Quill threw the gearshift into second, clipping a crate of ice that exploded like shattered diamonds in the rear-view glass.

Shanghai, 1939 – the year the Bund fog choked on Japanese gunpowder and Kansas City sax wails, Tiangzifang alleys twisting like opium pipes into eternity's dive.

"Miss longer, Quill," Slutpuppy's dice laugh echoed from the radiator jazz.

Hey Hey Club hid behind a silk-scroll door: Kansas City refugees—Count Basie strays, ex-Poteen Place horn-men fleein' Prohibition ghosts—blowin' bebop wild on tenor saxes and upright bass. Opium kingpin Dragon Joe slithered in with Black Orchid. Hey Hey erupted in 5/4 chaos: shots whorehouse-short, mirrors shatterin' a thousand guilty Orchids.

"Share," Orchid laughed dice-felt. Full jury eternal.

Dawn over Suzhou Creek. Burned the remnant bills sink-blue, pocketed her reed-souvenir, and boarded the ghost steamer solo. Next stop: Kansas City rails.

Havana, 1940 – the year the Malecón fog sucked rum and revolution, mambo horns wailing like widows over ledger corpses. SS Oriente ghosted into harbor, salt-crust shedding Tiangzifang haze like old skin.

"Sans Souci. KC exiles fence Frisco pages. Silky cigars from Oriente. Midnight. Fats."

Sans Souci throbbed Vedado velvet: mambo mania, Batista gorillas tuxed. El Tigre prowled with Cigar Lily. The satchel traded under the table until the gorillas drew and the 5/4 blaze took the mirrors. Luger lead-silk, Rico shotgun leak-black.

"Mambo slower," the note whispered.

Dawn nicotine in Habana Vieja. Burned the bills sink-blue, pocketed the cigar-reed. Ticket clerk yawned: "Lawless or eternity?" — "Kansas dust." The ghost steamer whistled low. Fog rum-slick eternal.
