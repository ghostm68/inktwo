DOCUMENTARY CONTEXT:
All musical cues are drawn from Johann Sebastian Bach (Sonatas & Partitas for Solo Violin, and the Double Violin Concerto in D Minor, BWV 1043). The narrative frame is a 1965 Berlin screening of the Czechoslovak film Atentát. Reinhard Heydrich is ambushed in Prague-Libeň on 27 May 1942 and dies at Bulovka at about 4:30 a.m. on 4 June 1942 — the eighth day. Passages from Lina Heydrich, Leben mit einem Kriegsverbrecher (Ludwig, 1976), are set verbatim in documentary annotation boxes.

[SCORE: Silence, save for the rhythmic, low CHATTER of a 35mm projector.]

A stark cone of projector light cuts through cigarette haze toward a canvas screen. The house is half full. Coats stay on. Someone coughs and does not apologize.

ON SCREEN (IN 35MM BLACK-AND-WHITE): Sequens’s Atentát (1964). A low camera tracks an open dark Mercedes-Benz slowing around the hairpin curve in Prague-Libeň. Two men in trench coats step from the curb. Tram wires cross above them like a stave.

IN THE AUDITORIUM: Sitting isolated in row six is LINA HEYDRICH (54). Severe wool coat, hair drawn back into a plain knot. She has not taken the seat on the aisle. She has taken the seat with a clear view of the exit. The flickering silver light sharpens her jawline. A paper program lies unopened on her lap.

ON SCREEN: The paratrooper raises a Sten gun. Pulls the trigger. CLICK. The bolt jams. In the row behind Lina, a man leans forward.

IN THE AUDITORIUM: Lina does not flinch. Her gloved fingers rise to her throat. She touches a strand of dull FRESHWATER PEARLS resting above her collar, rolling the center pearl against her collarbone — once, twice, the old habit. The glove leather creaks.

ON SCREEN: The second paratrooper hurls an anti-tank grenade. It shatters against the rear wheel housing. A DEAFENING BLAST. The auditorium flinches as one body. Lina’s pearl stops under her thumb.

DISSOLVE TO:
EXT. CASTLE JUNGFERN-BRESCHAN - COURTYARD - MORNING (MAY 27, 1942)

DAY ONE — THE MORNING HE LEAVES.

Blinding May sunlight. High lilacs in violet bloom, already dropping on the gravel. An open dark-green Mercedes 320 idles on the stones, the paint hot, the folded roof a black accordion behind the seats. A driver waits without looking at the house.

LINA (30), six months pregnant, stands on the terrace. Her apron bears faint traces of garden loam. Beside her, KLAUS (8) and HEIDER (7) chase each other across the grass, Klaus always half a length ahead, the smaller boy laughing at being beaten.

Lina catches Klaus by the wrist, gently wiping a streak of soil from his temple with her hem. He suffers it. He is already looking past her at the car.

LINA
Stay clear of the main gate, Klauschen. The trucks have blind corners.
KLAUS
Father promised to show me the new horse when he returns.
HEIDER
And me.
KLAUS
You are too small for that unruly mare.

Lina keeps Klaus’s wrist a second longer than the wipe required. Down at the gate, an SS sentry shifts his rifle and steals a quick glance at the children.

REINHARD HEYDRICH (38) steps from the double terrace doors. Field-grey tunic, cap tucked under his arm. The morning has already put a high color in his face. His long, pale hand rests over Lina’s shoulder. His fingers lightly brush the pearls at her neck, as if checking the clasp is still his.

HEYDRICH
Two days with the Führer in Berlin. Then straight home. Tell the groom to keep the mare in. Klaus pulls her mouth.
LINA
(hand lingering on his cuff)
Take the closed staff car, Reinhard. Just this once. The open car is impossible against the rain.
HEYDRICH
(a quiet, thin smile)
My Czechs know their master is not afraid. A roof invites ambush. The wind keeps the head clear.
LINA
The wind does not stop a stone.
HEYDRICH
Then the driver will see the stone.

He turns, touches Klaus’s blond hair, misses Heider’s reaching hand by an inch, and climbs into the front seat beside his driver, SS-Oberscharführer JOHANNES KLEIN. Not the back. Never the back. The Mercedes turns down the gravel drive. Lilac petals lift in the wake and settle.

Lina does not wave until the car is gone. Then she waves anyway, at the empty gate, the boys will .

follow her example
EXT. PRAGUE-LIBEŇ - JUNCTION OF KIRCHMAYEROVA AND V HOLEŠOVIČKÁCH - DAY (MAY 27, 1942 - 10:35 AM)

DAY ONE — 10:35 A.M. THE CURVE.

Dazzling midday glare. A sharp, downhill cobblestone curve where Kirchmayerova Avenue sweeps into V Holešovičkách. Overhead, electrical tram wires crisscross the sky. A woman with a string bag waits at the island. A baker’s van is somewhere up the hill, not yet in the story.

The dark-ash colored Mercedes-Benz 320 (SS-3) brakes hard to navigate the steep hairpin turn. Klein’s hands are low on the wheel. Heydrich sits beside him, cap off, the wind in his hair, a dispatch case between his boots.

Standing on the pavement curb: JOZEF GABČÍK (30). He drops his raincoat to the dirt, stepping straight into the roadway directly in front of the decelerating radiator grille. For a half-second he is only a man late for a tram.

In his hands: a British STEN MK II SUBMACHINE GUN. Gabčík braces the skeleton stock against his hip, takes aim point-blank at Heydrich’s chest, and squeezes the trigger.

A dry, hollow, metallic SNAP. The 9mm cartridge misfeeds. The bolt JAMS. Gabčík works it once. It will not come through.

In the front seat, Klein reaches for the floor shift to accelerate — standard escape protocol. The engine note rises.

Heydrich slaps Klein’s arm away with a violent backhand.

HEYDRICH
(screaming over the engine)
Halt! Sofort anhalten!

Klein stamps the floorboard brake. Tires SCREECH across the rails. The woman with the string bag steps backward into the tram shelter and stays there.

Instead of ducking, Heydrich leaps to his feet inside the open car. He unholsters his WALTHER PPK (7.65mm) and chambers a round, barking with furious, arrogant disdain. The man in the road is, to him, an error to be corrected in person.

Behind the car, JAN KUBIŠ lunges from behind a streetlamp. He swings an arm like a bowler, hurling a MODIFIED NO. 73 ANTI-TANK GRENADE.

The bomb strikes the right rear wheel well.

A CATACLYSMIC ROAR.

The blast punches the rear of the Mercedes off its suspension. The right side-panel disintegrates into flying metal, wheel splinters, pulverized seat padding, and coarse upholstery horsehair. Shrapnel cuts Kubiš across the brow; blood blinds his left eye as he staggers backward. Heydrich is thrown against the windscreen frame and does not yet understand that he has been hurt.

Acrid, gray-black smoke engulfs the junction. Passing tram No. 3 jerks to a screeching halt. Passengers hit the floorboards inside the cars. A bell keeps ringing because the driver is on the floor with the bell cord under his knee.

Inside the smoke, there is no heroic pause. Only frantic, ugly desperation.

Gabčík tosses the dead Sten into the gutter, spins on his heel, and sprints up the Kirchmayerova hill toward the tram island.

Through the swirling cordite smoke, the passenger door violently KICKS open.

HEYDRICH EMERGES ON HIS FEET.

His uniform is shredded at the lower flank; his tunic is blackened by blast soot. Flooded with massive combat adrenaline, he feels no pain yet — only animal rage. Crinoline and steel are already in the spleen. He does not know their names.

With his Walther PPK extended in his right hand, Heydrich SPRINTS into the intersection, pursuing Gabčík uphill.

CRASH! SMASH! THRASH!

Heydrich fires three wild rounds at Gabčík’s retreating back. Pedestrians scream and scatter behind lampposts. A window above a tobacconist opens and shuts again.

Gabčík pulls a COLT M1903 POCKET HAMMERLESS from his pocket, spinning mid-stride to snap two return shots. The bullets ricochet off the cobblestones near Heydrich’s boots.

Heydrich raises his Walther to fire a fourth round — CLICK. The pistol jams, or the magazine is spent. He looks at the gun as if it has disobeyed him.

Fifteen paces up the hill, the adrenaline shock cliff drops out from underneath him.

Heydrich STUMBLES. His face drains instantly from furious red to a hideous, wet, chalky grey. A deep, guttural groan rips from his throat.

His left hand clamps over his lower left flank. Dark, venous blood wells between his long fingers. Internally, his 11th rib is shattered, his diaphragm torn, his spleen pulverized. He cannot draw a full breath. His legs turn to water.

Heydrich staggers backward down the incline, clutching lampposts and tram poles to stay upright, dragging his boots until he reaches the shattered Mercedes.

He collapses chest-first over the warm steel of the hood, choking on his own blood. A tooth clicks against the paint.

Driver Klein runs around the vehicle, service pistol drawn, panicked.

KLEIN
Herr Obergruppenführer!
HEYDRICH
(coughing, spray of blood hitting the radiator)
Fassen Sie den Hund! Den Kerl... get him!

Klein turns and sprints up Kirchmayerova, chasing Gabčík into Brauner’s butcher shop (řeznictví) — where Gabčík will shoot Klein twice in the thigh and escape into the alleys. The shop bell rings. Then two shots. Then the bell again.

Left completely alone in the smoke, Heydrich slides off the hood onto the filthy cobblestones.

His fingers claw weakly at his waistband. The architect of the Police State lies bleeding in the gutter of an occupied capital. A passing delivery truck will haul him to Bulovka Hospital. He will not die today. He will take eight days.

HISTORICAL & FORENSIC DOSSIER — BULOVKA TO LIDICE:

1. The Pursuit: Contemporary police reports, eyewitness depositions, and the official Gestapo investigation (the Pannwitz Report) confirm that Heydrich was not immobilized in the vehicle. Driven by acute adrenaline, he exited the shattered car and chased Jozef Gabčík uphill, firing his Walther PPK until his weapon failed and internal hemorrhage caused circulatory collapse.

2. Eight Days: Wounded 27 May 1942. Operated on at Bulovka by Dr. Josef Hohlbaum and Prof. Dr. Walter Dick, later attended by Himmler’s physician Karl Gebhardt. Fever and wound drainage through 2 June. A false rally. Around noon on 3 June, while sitting up to eat, he collapses into shock and coma. He dies at about 4:30 a.m. on 4 June — day eight. Bulovka death register Nr. 348/1942: wound infection after the attempt. Forensics point to massive diaphragmatic contamination, retroperitoneal hematoma, and pulmonary embolism. Penicillin was not available to him.

3. Totalitarian Reprisal (Lidice, June 10, 1942): Heydrich’s death triggered the savage logic of Nazi terror. Acting under Hitler’s direct order to “wade through blood,” the SS surrounded the mining village of Lidice on the night of June 9. On June 10, all 173 men and boys over fifteen were executed against a barn wall; 184 women were deported to Ravensbrück; 88 children were murdered in the gas vans of Chełmno. The village was burned, dynamited, and plowed flat. Ležáky was destroyed on 24 June.

4. Lina Heydrich and the Reprisals: Lina held no command and was not consulted — consumed by the state funeral, grief, and late pregnancy (Marta was born six weeks later). She was certainly aware of Lidice. Unlike the death camps, Lidice was boasted of on the radio and across Prague in red posters and front pages. In her postwar memoirs she compartmentalized it: because Reinhard died six days before the village was razed, she dismissed Lidice as Hitler’s personal revenge and disclaimed any moral connection between her family and the revenge.

Before the empire, before the black coats and the dossiers, there was only a cashiered sailor sitting in his mother’s drafty parlor with his ego cut to ribbons.
DISSOLVE TO:
INT. HEYDRICH FAMILY APARTMENT, HALLE - NIGHT (APRIL 1931)

[SCORE: A solitary upright piano plays J.S. BACH — SARABANDE FROM PARTITA IN B MINOR, haltingly, from down the hall.]

Rain beats against the tall windowpanes. Worn velvet armchairs. The wallpaper shows pale patches where family portraits were sold during the hyperinflation. A metronome sits stopped on the sideboard, its arm raised and useless.

From the music room, BRUNO HEYDRICH — composer, conservatory man, creditor to half of Halle — plays eight bars, stops, plays them again worse. He does not come in.

REINHARD (27) sits at the table in an ill-fitting civilian suit. His naval buttons have been sheared off. The thread holes show. He stares down at his empty palms as if the rank might still be in them.

LINA (19), sharp-eyed, already a Party member two years, pours boiling water into a stone teapot. She does not ask him how he is. She has decided how he will be.

HEYDRICH
Discharged for dishonor. The Admiral signed the paper without looking up. Ten years of service erased because an admiral’s daughter took offense. My father cannot walk past the conservatory without neighbors whispering.
LINA
Your father lives in a thirty years ago past. We live now. Look at me, Reinhard.

She steps to him, taking his cold hands. The piano down the hall stops mid-phrase. Heydrich pulls a small, scuffed leather case from his vest. Snaps it open.

Inside rests a delicate strand of freshwater pearls. Not grand. River pearls, slightly irregular, the kind a singer hides when the silver goes.

HEYDRICH
My mother’s father gave them to her when she sang at the Dresden Opera. When the inflation wiped out the school, they sold the silver forks. She hid these in the piano case. She told me to sell them to pay our rent in Kiel.

He lifts the necklace. The dull pearls catch the gas lamp’s glow. Down the hall, Bruno starts the sarabande a third time, angry at the left hand.

HEYDRICH (CONT'D)
They stripped my rank. They made me walk through the naval gates like a vagrant. I swear to you, Lina... they will never take anything from us again.

His long fingers reach behind her neck, fastening the silver clasp. The cold pearls settle firmly against her skin. She does not tell him the clasp is too tight. She will loosen it later, when he is asleep.

LINA
(touching the clasp)
Then go to Munich. Let this man Himmler see what you can build. A cashiered sailor is still a sailor. A file is only paper.
LINA HEYDRICH — LEBEN MIT EINEM KRIEGSVERBRECHER (1976):
“Als Reinhard im April 1931 aus der Reichsmarine entlassen wurde, brach für ihn eine Welt zusammen. Er saß tagelang im Zimmer seiner Eltern in Halle, den Kopf in die Hände gestützt, und sah keine Zukunft mehr. Das Urteil des Ehrengerichts hatte ihn vernichtet; er fühlte sich ausgestoßen und ehrlos. Ich war es, die ihn aufrichtete. Ich sagte ihm: ‚Wir fangen von vorne an. Geh nach München zu diesem Himmler. Zeig ihnen, wer du bist.‘ Aus dieser Verzweiflung heraus ist alles entstanden, was später kam.”

[Translation: When Reinhard was dismissed from the Reichsmarine in April 1931, his world collapsed. He sat for days in his parents’ room in Halle, head in his hands, seeing no future. The verdict of the court of honor had destroyed him; he felt outcast and stripped of honor. It was I who raised him up. I told him: ‘We start anew. Go to Munich to this man Himmler. Show them who you are.’ Out of that despair arose everything that followed.]
INT. APARTMENT ON TÜRKENSTRASSE, MUNICH - BEDROOM - NIGHT (JUNE 17, 1933)

[SCORE: Silence, save for the rhythmic clatter of a late-night streetcar below.]

Sweltering June heat. A cramped fourth-floor attic room with peeling floral wallpaper. A washbasin. Two marks and some pfennigs on the dresser. His SS cap on a chair, still new enough that the sweatband is pale.

LINA (22) lies on a narrow iron cot, hair drenched and plastered to her face. She screams through clenched teeth, her fingers digging violently into the mattress ticking as a contraction tears through her. Eighty marks a month from Himmler. Cabbage in the pot. This.

Beside her on the floor, HEYDRICH (29) is on his knees in an undershirt and suspenders, his tie tossed over a chair. He has just come from the Brown House. His knuckles are white, holding Lina’s hand against his jaw. He is shaking. The man who will build a police state does not know where to put his eyes.

HEYDRICH
(whispering frantically, terrified)
Hold onto me, Lina. Don't look away. Look at me. Squeeze my hand. Squeeze it as hard as you can.

A local MIDWIFE in a gray apron pulls back the sheet. She has seen richer rooms and worse husbands.

MIDWIFE
Now, Frau Heydrich. With the pain. Now!

Lina rears upward, pulling Reinhard toward her with an animal groan. Her collar pulls open — the freshwater pearls glint against her sweat-slick skin. She will not let the midwife take them off.

A wet, sharp GASP, followed by a piercing, furious NEWBORN CRY.

Lina collapses back into the pillows, her chest heaving. For a moment the only sound is the streetcar and the child, arguing with the world.

The midwife cuts the cord, wrapping the squirming infant in a rough flour sack. She places the baby — KLAUS — onto Lina’s bare breast.

Heydrich buries his face in the hollow of Lina’s shoulder. His shoulders convulse — weeping in sheer, unvarnished relief. He presses kiss after kiss into her damp neck, brushing the pearls. The midwife looks away, not from modesty, from surprise.

HEYDRICH
A boy, Lina. A boy... Klaus. Our Klaus.
LINA (V.O.)
We had eighty marks a month from Himmler. We had two rooms and cabbage in the pot. But on that night, he was just my husband, trembling on the floor with our son's breath on his skin. I thought that was what we were fighting for. I did not understand that the uniform eats the man, stitch by stitch, until there is nothing left inside.

FIVE YEARS LATER. Total contrast. The cry becomes a violin entrance and stays a violin.

A fire blazes in the brick hearth. Snow ticks the tall windows and melts before it can matter. HEYDRICH, now in the black SS uniform with silver collar runes, stands shoulder-to-shoulder with ERIKA CANARIS. Their bows rise and descend in razor precision. He plays with closed eyes, his narrow profile lit by the orange hearth. The tenderness he will not spend on men he spends here, on the beat.

On the sofa, ADMIRAL WILHELM CANARIS sips Mosel wine from crystal and watches his wife’s bow, not Heydrich’s. He has already decided he does not trust the tempo.

KLAUS
(whispering)
Look, Mother. The train is going through the mountain.
LINA
(kissing his temple)
Quietly, my heart. Listen to your father’s bow.

The music pauses on an exquisite, suspended cadence. Heydrich lowers the instrument, his eyes opening with detached clarity. Erika waits, bow up, for a man who has already left the piece.

CANARIS
If our ministries could find that same resolution, Reinhard, Europe might sleep past dawn.
HEYDRICH
Dissonance is only permitted when it leads to a decisive conclusion, Admiral. Otherwise, it is simply noise.
CANARIS
(a small toast that is not a toast)
And who decides the conclusion?
HEYDRICH
The man who is still playing.

Lina looks from her husband’s tunic down to Klaus, who has fallen asleep across her lap, a wooden carriage still in his fist. Her hand touches the pearls at her neck. The same clasp. A different room. Erika begins to loosen her bow hair. Canaris watches the sleeping child as if counting something.

LINA (V.O.)
He put every ounce of tenderness he had left into Bach. For the world, he saved the iron. I told myself the home was ours, and the iron was merely the wall keeping the wilderness out. I did not ask who had built the wilderness.
LINA HEYDRICH — LEBEN MIT EINEM KRIEGSVERBRECHER (1976):
When I then travelled with the children from Berlin to Prague, it was in a saloon carriage. Never before had I travelled so “finely.” I sensed what was coming. At first the fuss the railway made of me seemed a little strange; but I got used to it quickly. I did not come to terms with Prague so quickly. From the main station we drove in a column of cars to Prague Castle. Barriers, police along the roadside. I only came to myself again when I was standing at a window of the castle, looking down on Prague shimmering gold. Lofty feelings took hold of me: I am no longer “a person,” I felt. I am a princess, and I live in a fairy-tale land. There is no war, no enemies, no differences. I am standing in the middle of God’s garden, and I am allowed to experience, to look, to enjoy. Then I think of the history of this city of fate, in which, for me, all the political, national and emotional threads run together: Prague is now Europe for me. How hard and heavy the time is, I learn more from the newspapers than from Reinhard. A state of emergency, martial law, a curfew in the evening. My husband is sweeping here with a terrible iron broom. In the first week he used up three adjutants. They were simply not equal to the strain. He himself did not get out of his clothes for a week. So I grasp relatively quickly that I am no princess, only the schoolteacher’s daughter Lina von Osten from the island of Fehmarn, whom nobody knows. I, who now stand as “mistress of the castle” at a window of the Hradčany, am after all only a tool without weight. But I must fulfil my “duty,” and I may not live out my existence merely for the sake of the outward appearance. I, the wife of Reinhard Heydrich, must grasp it inwardly and affirm it. So I now try to fit myself in and to take my place, and I notice very soon that much is not as I would like it. The rooms do not please me. To have history always in front of one, behind one and around one does not suit my nature. For Reinhard everything is quite different. He lives only in rooms that inspire him. For him the reality is wholly there. Problems are what the Deputy Reich Protector Heydrich has..]
INT. BULOVKA HOSPITAL, PRAGUE - 3RD FLOOR CORRIDOR - DAY (MAY 31, 1942)

DAY FOUR. THE TREACHEROUS RALLY BEGINS.

[SCORE: J.S. BACH — SOLO VIOLIN PARTITA IN D MINOR. A single, brittle, high-register cadence, like ice fracturing underfoot.]

Sunlight streams through the high hospital windows. SS sentries in black uniforms stand at rigid attention every six paces. A nurse carries a basin and is stopped, checked, waved on. The corridor smells of soap and hot wool.

PROFESSOR DR. WALTER DICK (42), chief surgeon of Bulovka — a man destined to survive the Reich, the collapse, and the expulsion, and to practice surgery in West Germany until old age — stands by the glass nursing partition. He taps a medical chart with a silver pencil. The pencil does not believe the afternoon.

DR. DICK (V.O.)
In trauma surgery, we learn to fear the third day more than the first. We give it a German name: Die trügerische Besserung. The Treacherous Rally. The body, flooded with its final reserves, stages an exquisite pantomime of health. The fever drops. The cheeks flush. The patient smiles. Families send for clean shirts.
INT. PRIVATE CORNER ROOM - CONTINUOUS

HEYDRICH sits fully upright against white bolster pillows. The drains have been pulled from his left side, or he has demanded they be discreet. His shaved cheeks are clean; his voice, usually high and reedy, carries authority across the room. Day four. He has decided he is winning. The fencer is taking a forward stance.

On his bed tray: a bowl of clear meat broth, dark rye bread, and butter. A leather dispatch folder from Hradčany, string untied.

Lina sits close beside him on the iron chair, holding his sleeve. For the first time in four days, her shoulders are loose. Her face glows with the fierce, triumphant relief of a mother whose house has survived the hurricane. The unborn child turns under her dress. She puts his hand there without asking.

LINA
Eat the crust, Reinhard. You need the starch for the wound to knit. Klaus asked if he may come tomorrow. I told him Thursday, if the chart holds.

Heydrich takes the spoon from her hand. He chews slowly, leafing through the dispatch folder with his free hand. A name is underlined. He does not show her the page.

HEYDRICH
Tell Karl Hermann Frank to execute the hostages in groups of ten. Not fifty. It creates paperwork. Tell him to have the car repaired. I want the same chassis.
LINA
(a beat; she does not ask which hostages)
The same chassis. I heard you.

He reaches out, his long, aristocratic fingers resting affectionately over Lina’s rounded stomach, feeling the unborn child. He smiles. It is the smile of an indestructible titan at the apex of Europe. Outside this room, in May 1942, the front is still a boast.

In the corridor doorway, Dr. Dick watches the scene, holding the temperature chart.

DR. DICK (V.O.)
In that sunlit room in May 1942, they believed they were gods. Hitler was at the gates of the Caucasus; Rommel was outside Egypt; Europe was theirs for a thousand years. To whisper in that room that exactly three years later — between May 2nd and May 8th, 1945 — their Berlin would be a smoldering cemetery of shell craters, with Hitler’s corpse haunting a destroyed Berlin and Soviet tanks grinding over the Wilhelmstrasse... it would have sounded like the ranting of a lunatic.

CLOSE ON: The temperature chart in Dr. Dick’s hands. Beneath the normal line, the pulse graph is already ticking upward: 110... 120... 130. A silent, runaway engine. Day four, pretending to be recovery.

DR. DICK (V.O.) (CONT'D)
The crinoline and the blast dirt were already devouring the diaphragm from within. The Reich was Heydrich, and Heydrich was the Reich: bloated with conquest, oblivious to peril, eating broth in bed while the rot was already eating the heart.
DR. DICK (V.O.) (CONT'D)
Maybe he believed what the propaganda posters said about him: that an Aryan will could master biology. He demanded bread when his gut needed silence; he sat at attention when his heart needed rest. He did not know how to be sick. He fought his own body like it was an occupied province — until the province finally burned down around him.
INT. BULOVKA HOSPITAL, PRAGUE - PRIVATE ROOM - DUSK (JUNE 2, 1942)

DAY SIX. FEVER. THE WOUND STILL DRAINING.

[SCORE: Bach’s Chaconne slows, dropping into a low, mournful drone.]

A sterile, shadowy room. Blackout paper on the lower half of the window. SS boots in the corridor, not marching — waiting. Heydrich lies propped on white pillows, thick bandages swathing his torso, a glass drain tube disappearing under the gauze. His cheeks are gray with fever sweat. The smell is carbolic and meat broth.

Lina sits beside the bed, holding a bowl carried from their kitchen. The hospital soup has already been refused. She blows on a spoonful until the steam thins.

LINA
The fever will break tonight, Reinhard. The boys made drawings of the stable for you. Klaus put the mare in the wrong field. Heider corrected him in red pencil.

Heydrich’s dry lips move faintly. His eyes are on the ceiling, not on her.

HEYDRICH
Denk an unsere Kinder... Take them to Fehmarn if the front collapses.
LINA
Do not speak of collapses. You are the Protector. Himmler’s physician says the chart is turning.
HEYDRICH
(a breath that costs him)
Charts are for clerks.

His long fingers twitch on the bedsheet, tracing the silent fingerings of a violin passage — third position, then a drop, then nothing. Lina sets the spoon back in the bowl. The broth skins over.

INT. PRIVATE ROOM - DAY (JUNE 3, 1942 - NOON)

DAY SEVEN. THE RALLY BREAKS.

SEVENTY-TWO HOURS LATER. The false dawn has lasted long enough for clean shirts and orders about chassis.

Heydrich sits at the midday meal. He lifts a porcelain cup of coffee to his lips. Lina is in the chair, a letter from Klaus half-read aloud. The word “mare” is still in the air.

Suddenly, his jaw clenches. The cup SLAMS onto the tray and shatters; dark coffee soaks the white sheets. Not a faint. A cave-in.

His eyes bulge, draining of color. His hand claws violently at his left flank. A sound like tearing canvas rips from his throat as his blood pressure craters.

LINA
Reinhard?! Reinhard!

SS doctors, orderlies, and Dr. Dick burst through the double doors. Alarm bells RING down the corridor. Gebhardt’s name is shouted and does not arrive in time to matter.

Heydrich violently arches his back, drowning in acute septic shock, his fingernails tearing the mattress linen as he slips into the terminal coma. A spoon hits the floor and spins.

Lina is shoved backward against the wall by four SS surgeons. Her hands fly to her throat, clutching the freshwater pearls as the monitors BEAM. She keeps saying his name after the room has stopped using it.

INT. PRIVATE ROOM - NIGHT INTO DAWN (JUNE 3–4, 1942)

DAY EIGHT. HE DOES NOT WAKE.

The coma holds. Night drains the color out of the SS uniforms in the doorway until they are only shapes. Lina refuses the chair. She stands. Then she is given a cot and does not lie on it. A clock in the corridor ticks louder than the breathing.

Around 4:30 in the morning of 4 June, the breathing changes register — wet, then thin, then absent. No violin fingerings. No order about the chassis. Dr. Dick looks at the clock because someone will ask him for the minute.

Lina waits for the sentence she will later say he spoke — that she should come again soon. The room does not give it to her. She will put it in the memoir anyway. The pearls are cutting into her palm. She does not open the hand.

DR. DICK (V.O.)
Eight days from the curve to this clock. The posters had promised a man who could not be killed by a province. The province was his own spleen.
HISTORICAL DOSSIER — PROF. DR. WALTER DICK & THE MAY PARALLEL:

1. The Surviving Surgeon: Unlike Karl Gebhardt (hanged at Nuremberg in 1948) or Josef Hohlbaum, Prof. Dr. Walter Dick (1899–1990) survived the war. As the Sudeten German chief of surgery at Bulovka, he performed Heydrich’s initial laparotomy alongside Czech anesthetist Dr. Alois Honěk. After 1945, Dick was expelled to West Germany, serving as a professor of surgery at the University of Tübingen until retirement.

2. The Treacherous Interval (May 1942 → May 1945): Between the operation and 2 June 1942, Heydrich experienced a classic false dawn. Lina and the SS treated the crisis as passed. In late spring 1942 the Nazi empire stood at its geographic peak. That precisely three years later — 2–8 May 1945 — Berlin would fall was as unthinkable in that room as his collapse at noon on 3 June and his death at about 4:30 a.m. on 4 June.

3. The Spoken Farewell: Lina later claimed he briefly woke and asked her to visit again soon. Clinically implausible; she was heavily sedated and Heydrich was comatose. Recorded as her protective myth, not as the room’s reality.
LINA HEYDRICH — LEBEN MIT EINEM KRIEGSVERBRECHER (1976):
“In the night of 3–4 June I am torn abruptly from sleep. I am to come to the hospital at once. A bad turn has set in. The night is cold; I am freezing. My teeth chatter. My hands tremble. My bag falls to the floor of the car, where I simply leave it. Everything now seems so unimportant to me. Then I am standing before my husband’s bed. He is already in a coma. They lead me away and give me a sedative injection. When I wake again, Reinhard is dead. It is 4 June 1942, ten in the morning, when the radio announces: the Obergruppenführer and General of Police, Chief of the Secret State Police and of the Reich Security Service, the Deputy Reich Protector of Bohemia and Moravia, Reinhard Heydrich, has today succumbed to his injuries. State mourning is ordered; for the funeral ceremonies in Prague and Berlin an expenditure is set in motion such as has not been known before. The state is bearing a faithful servant to the grave. In the Invalids’ Cemetery in Berlin he receives a simple soldier’s grave with a wooden cross, on which his steel helmet is set. He had always felt himself a soldier, and so he died as well. Because I feel ill and wretched, and am approaching my confinement, our two sons Klaus and Heider take part in my place in the funeral ceremony in the Reich Chancellery. Later she returns to the bed itself: I was notified only when it seemed certain that the immediate danger of death had been averted. Soon welcome and unwelcome visitors appeared, though most of them were sent back into the corridor. Himmler had at once dispatched his SS doctor, Gebhardt. Reinhard was too ill to raise objections to anything. I know that there was, for example, much he would gladly have held against Gebhardt; but in the few days still granted him, he reconciled himself with Gebhardt. Reinhard’s words to me on his deathbed, “Go back to Fehmarn,” have never let me go. Had he sensed his approaching death? His whole behaviour in this time set me questions and riddles. Suddenly I no longer knew who the man was to whom I had been married for almost eleven years. Whether the composure with which he now bore his fate was a product of the sedatives and narcotics, or came from his attitude, I could not determine. Today I incline to believe that he affirmed this end. He had no fear, but also not the will to go on living, which seemed incomprehensible to me in this bundle of energy and strength of will. It seemed to me that he had long since made his peace with the idea of dying early. Apparently he “knew” that he, who decided over the life and death of other people, would soon himself be presented with such a “bill.” Again and again he had only said, when I begged him to give up his “profession”: “You don’t understand. I must do it. Only I can; the others cannot.” Certainly it sounds like a phrase; but I believe he wanted to sacrifice himself. His superfluous, and in part even order-defying, flying missions seem to me today to have been the beginning. That Reinhard loved us, the family, very much, he attested once more in his farewell letter, which was handed to me after his death. His last words on the morning of 4 June 1942, after he had woken once more from a long unconsciousness: “Oh, you are here, how lovely. Come again in the afternoon. You will come again, won’t you?” When Himmler visited him on 2 June, he is said to have received him with great composure, even with a little cheerfulness. What the two men said to each other, nobody knows. Of Himmler I know only that Reinhard recited to him this verse from the opera Das Leierkind, written by his father: “Yes, the world is only a barrel organ, which our Lord God himself turns, and everyone must dance to the song that happens to be on the roller.”on: When Reinhard was dismissed from the Reichsmarine in April 1931, his world collapsed. He sat for days in his parents’ room in Halle, head in his hands, seeing no future. The verdict of the court of honor had destroyed him; he felt outcast and stripped of honor. It was I who raised him up. I told him: ‘We start anew. Go to Munich to this man Himmler. Show them who you are.’ Out of that despair arose everything that followed.]
INT. MOSAIC HALL, NEW REICH CHANCELLERY, BERLIN - DAY (JUNE 9, 1942)

FIVE DAYS AFTER THE DEATH. THE STATE HAS THE BODY.

[SCORE: A massive organ blasts WAGNER'S FUNERAL MARCH, hollow and deafening, obliterating all intimacy.]

A towering cavern of red marble, polished granite, and giant vertical swastika standards. Wreaths of blood-red carnations stacked ten feet high. The coffin has already lain in the courtyard of Prague Castle, where tens of thousands filed past. Now it belongs to Berlin.

At the front of the hall, isolated before the massive bronze catafalque, stand KLAUS (8) and HEIDER (7), both dressed in tailored black velvet sailor suits. Klaus’s collar itches. He will not scratch.

Their mother is absent — two hundred miles away, confined to bed in Jungfern-Breschan.

ADOLF HITLER approaches along the marble runner, his face set in theatrical lamentation. The organ covers the sound of his boots until he is close enough to smell. He stops before the motherless boys.

Hitler extends his black-gloved hands. He cups Klaus’s trembling jaw, tilting the eight-year-old’s face upward toward the bronze ceiling. The glove is warm from another handshake.

HITLER
Your father had an iron heart. You will also be iron for the Reich.

Klaus’s chin quivers under the leather glove. Terrified, with no hand to hold, his eyes dart across the towering sea of black uniforms. Heider stares at Hitler’s moustache as if it were exotic.

Hitler withdraws his glove, turning toward the velvet cushion before the casket, where decorations will be laid on a dead man who can no longer use them.

INTERCUT WITH:
INT. CASTLE JUNGFERN-BRESCHAN - BEDROOM - DAY (JUNE 9, 1942)

LINA lies alone in the darkened bedroom, heavily pregnant, pale and shivering beneath the duvet. On the nightstand, the Volksempfänger radio blares the organ chords and Hitler's hollow voice from Berlin.

Her fingers grip the sheet until the threads strain.

LINA (V.O.)
Because I was ill and wretched, approaching my confinement, my two boys took my place in Berlin. Two hundred miles away, through the radio speaker, I heard what they had done. Reinhard was no longer my husband. He was no longer the father who lifted Klaus onto the mare. He had been eaten by the State theater. My boys stood like soldiers while Hitler honored our man at the center of the world.

She does not yet know that tonight, and tomorrow, Lidice will be erased under the same order that dressed this hall. The radio will boast of it. She will hear the boast. She will put it in a different room of the house.

“Weil ich mich elend und krank fühlte und meiner Niederkunft entgegensing, nahmen unsere beiden Söhne Klaus und Heider an meiner Stelle an dem Staatsakt in der Reichskanzlei teil. Sie standen dort ganz allein unter den mächtigen Männern, zwei kleine Knaben in Matrosenanzügen, während Hitler ihnen die Hände auflegte. Ich hörte die Übertragung im Radio in Jungfern-Breschan und weinte vor Hilflosigkeit... Die spätere Zerstörung von Lidice war Hitlers persönliche Rache. Reinhard war bereits seit sechs Tagen tot. [Translation: Because I felt ill and wretched, and was approaching my confinement, our two sons Klaus and Heider took part in my place in the funeral ceremony in the Reich Chancellery. They stood there completely alone among the powerful men, two little boys in sailor suits, while Hitler laid his hands upon them. I heard the broadcast on the radio in Jungfern-Breschan and wept from helplessness... The subsequent destruction of Lidice was Hitler's personal revenge. Reinhard had already been dead for six days. EXT. CASTLE JUNGFERN-BRESCHAN - ENTRANCE GATE - LATE AFTERNOON (OCTOBER 24, 1943)

SIXTEEN MONTHS LATER. A child's bicycle lies crushed in the gravel, its front wheel bent sideways. The black mare is not in this shot. A Czech lorry idles, radiator ticking, the driver already out and useless.

Lina falls to her knees on the road. She gathers KLAUS (10) into her lap. The boy’s blond hair is soaked in crimson; his eyes are fixed on the clouds. One shoe is still on. The other is under the truck. She does not look for it yet.

LINA
(a raw, broken choke)
Klaus... Klauschen... Mother is here. Look at Mother. You were going to the gate. I told you about the gate.

The Czech lorry driver stands by his fender, palms pressed to his eyes. SS sentries run forward with rifles raised, as if a rifle could sort this.

LINA (CONT'D)
(screaming at the guards)
DON’T TOUCH HIM! LEAVE US ALONE!

She rocks once. The pearls knock against Klaus’s cheek. He does not feel them. Somewhere in the house, Heider has been told not to come to the window. He comes to the window.

[SCORE: Silence falls instantly, total and dry. The only sound is the cold Baltic wind.]

LINA (V.O.)
They said the paratroopers killed Reinhard. But Klaus... Klaus was taken by a lorry on a sunlit afternoon. And that was when I knew: no uniform, no castle, no empire could keep anything safe. I did not then ask what the empire had failed to keep safe for other mothers.
DISSOLVE TO:
EXT. FEHMARN ISLAND - BALTIC SHORE - DUSK (1960)

[SCORE: J.S. BACH — SOLO VIOLIN PARTITA NO. 2 (CHACONNE) enters again, distant, carried on the sound of crashing waves.]

Grey dunes. Sea grass whipping in the autumn gale. The weathered guesthouse Imbria Parva stands solitary against the grey horizon — her inn, her argument with the century, tables to be set before dark. A gull screams and is not answered.

LINA (49) walks along the water’s edge in heavy boots and a wind-whipped woolen coat. The white foam churns over dark rocks. She has a letter in her pocket from a court, or from a journalist, or from both; she has not opened it on the beach. Salt would spoil the stamp.

Her hand reaches into her collar, rolling the freshwater pearls between her bare fingers. One pearl has yellowed. She knows which one.

LINA (V.O.)
Sometimes, when the salt wind rattles the windows, it seems to me that it was all an operatic hallucination. Did those twelve years in Berlin and Prague ever exist? The red marble, the grand pianos, the firing squads, the telephone ringing at dawn... It feels like a nightmare dreamt by someone else.

She looks out toward the grey sea, her jaw set like stone. A fishing boat makes no progress against the chop and does not turn back.

LINA (V.O.) (CONT'D)
They come from the courts with their stamps and subpoenas. They want me to spit on his memory so they can forgive themselves. But if I say he was vile, then Klaus’s laughter was a lie. The violin was a lie. These pearls around my neck are the only proof that any of it was real. He was a soldier who held the line assigned to him. The rest... was the storm.

She believes the last two sentences. The beach does not argue. That is the danger of the scene, and it should be left dangerous.

Lina pushes into the cold autumn night. Wet asphalt reflects the neon lights of the divided city. A tram bell somewhere, not this street’s.

Beside her sits Reinhard. In profile, his sharp chin is lifted toward the vaulted ceiling, listening to his dead father’s notes fill the palace of a Bohemian prince.

Beneath the edge of her dark silk program, his bare hand slides across the velvet seat. His fingers lace into hers. Warm. A slight tremor in his pulse.

Not the architect of the camps. Not the butcher on the posters. Just a nervous musician’s son holding his wife’s hand in the dark. The film knows both things are true. She will only keep one.

Beneath the dark silk of her gown, her belly shifts. The unborn child — Marta — kicks sharply against the fabric. Reinhard doesn't turn his head, but his thumb presses firmly into Lina’s palm. A private, conspiratorial squeeze.

LINA (V.O.)
They think we were thinking of flags. We were not thinking of flags. We were thinking of an attic in Halle where an old man couldn't pay his gas bill, while his music echoed through the palace of kings. His hand was warm. His seed was moving under my ribs. For one hour, before the sun rose and the car left the gates, the world had no uniforms. It had only us.

EXT. FRIEDRICHSTRASSE, EAST BERLIN - NIGHT (1965)

Lina blinks. The palace, the music, the warmth of his fingers vanish.

There is only the cold Baltic mist, the wet asphalt of Friedrichstraße, and the empty grey wool of her coat. She turns up her collar and walks into the drizzle, solitary and erect.

Her thumb strokes the palm of her own bare hand — remembering the squeeze that ended twenty-three years ago yet never surrendered, never ceased.

She disappears into the mist. The street does not close behind her. Somewhere a projector is still running the crypt.
